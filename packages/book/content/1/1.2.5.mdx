<Prose>

The greatest common divisor (GCD) of two integers *a* and *b* is the largest integer that divides both of them with no remainder. The GCD of 16 and 28 is 4. In chapter 2, reducing a rational number to lowest terms will mean dividing its numerator and denominator by their GCD.

One way to find it is to factor both numbers and look for common factors, but there is a famous algorithm that is much more efficient. If *r* is the remainder when *a* is divided by *b*, then the common divisors of *a* and *b* are exactly the common divisors of *b* and *r*. So GCD(*a*, *b*) = GCD(*b*, *r*), and we can keep reducing until the second number is 0, at which point the first number is the answer. This is *Euclid's algorithm*.

</Prose>

<Example index={0} file="gcd.sicp" source={gcdProgram} anim="process">

GCD(206, 40) = GCD(40, 6) = GCD(6, 4) = GCD(4, 2) = GCD(2, 0) = 2. Each line of the rewriting replaces the pair by a smaller one and leaves nothing pending, so the process is iterative.

</Example>

<Prose>

The number of steps grows as the logarithm of the numbers involved. That follows from a theorem proved by Gabriel Lamé in 1845.

**Lamé's theorem.** If Euclid's algorithm requires *k* steps to compute the GCD of some pair, then the smaller number in the pair must be greater than or equal to the *k*th Fibonacci number.

Consecutive Fibonacci numbers are therefore the worst case. Let *n* be the smaller of the two inputs. If the process takes *k* steps, then *n* ≥ Fib(*k*) ≈ φᵏ / √5, so the number of steps grows as the logarithm to the base φ of *n*: Θ(log *n*). The measurements below run `gcd` on four Fibonacci pairs, each about eleven times larger than the last.

</Prose>

<Example index={1} file="lame.sicp" source={lameProgram} anim="growth">

Each pair is φ⁵ ≈ 11 times larger than the one before and costs exactly five more steps. On the logarithmic axis that is a straight line, which is what Θ(log *n*) looks like. Try other pairs: by the theorem, no pair whose smaller number is 55 can take more than ten steps.

</Example>

<Exercise id="1.20">

The process that a function generates depends on the rules the interpreter uses. Consider the iterative `gcd` above, and suppose it were interpreted with normal-order evaluation, as discussed in §1.1.5, with the conditional expression evaluated by its own rule as in §1.1.6. Using the substitution method for normal order, illustrate the process generated by `gcd(206, 40)` and count the remainder operations that are actually performed. How many are performed in the applicative-order evaluation of `gcd(206, 40)`?

<Solution>

**Solution.** In applicative order each step computes one remainder, so there are 4. In normal order the remainder expressions are substituted unevaluated, and each test `b === 0` evaluates its `b` from scratch. The five tests evaluate 0, 1, 2, 4 and 7 remainders before one finally succeeds, and the answer `a` then costs 4 more: 14 + 4 = 18. Section 4.2 builds a lazy evaluator that avoids this repetition by remembering each value once it is computed.

</Solution>
