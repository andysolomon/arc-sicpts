<Prose>

The last two sections showed processes that consume resources at very different rates. The notion of *order of growth* gives a coarse measure of the difference. Let *n* be a parameter that measures the size of a problem, and *R*(*n*) the amount of some resource the process needs for a problem of size *n*: steps, or the deepest stack of pending operations, or anything else we can count.

We say that *R*(*n*) has order of growth Θ(*f*(*n*)), written *R*(*n*) = Θ(*f*(*n*)), if there are positive constants *k*₁ and *k*₂, independent of *n*, such that *k*₁ *f*(*n*) ≤ *R*(*n*) ≤ *k*₂ *f*(*n*) for any sufficiently large *n*. In words: for large *n*, *R*(*n*) lies between two fixed multiples of *f*(*n*).

The recursive `factorial` of §1.2.1 takes Θ(*n*) steps and Θ(*n*) space. `fact_iter` also takes Θ(*n*) steps but only Θ(1) space. The tree-recursive `fib` takes Θ(φⁿ) steps and Θ(*n*) space. Nobody needs to take that on trust. The program below measures both functions at four sizes each, counting the calls each one makes (a stand-in for steps) and the deepest the stack of pending calls gets (a stand-in for space).

</Prose>

<Prose>

Orders of growth are crude. A process that takes *n*² steps, one that takes 1000*n*², and one that takes 3*n*² + 10*n* + 17 all have Θ(*n*²) order of growth. That crudeness is the point: the constant factors depend on the machine and on how the function was written, but the order of growth predicts how the process will behave when the problem changes size. For a Θ(*n*) process, doubling the size roughly doubles the resources used. For an exponential process, each increment in size multiplies the resources by a constant factor.

The rest of §1.2 looks at two algorithms whose order of growth is logarithmic, so that doubling the problem size adds only a constant amount of work.

</Prose>

<Exercise id="1.14">

Draw the tree of calls generated by `count_change(11)` from §1.2.2, on paper, and decide what the orders of growth of its space and number of steps are as the amount increases. Then make the count of steps checkable: write `cc_calls(amount, kinds_of_coins)`, which returns how many times `cc` is applied in evaluating `cc(amount, kinds_of_coins)`, without calling `cc`.

<Solution>

**Solution.** `cc_calls` has the shape of `cc` itself: a leaf costs one call, and any other call costs one plus the calls of its two branches. The tree for `count_change(11)` has 55 nodes. Space is Θ(*n*), because the deepest path is the one that pays the amount in pennies. Steps grow as Θ(*n*ᵏ) for *k* kinds of coin, Θ(*n*⁵) for the five here: with only pennies the tree is a path; each further kind of coin makes, at each of Θ(*n*) amounts along the way, a whole subtree of the previous kind.

</Solution>
</Exercise>
<Exercise id="1.15">

The sine of an angle *x* in radians can be computed using sin *x* ≈ *x* for small *x*, and the identity sin *x* = 3 sin(*x*/3) − 4 sin³(*x*/3) to reduce the size of the argument. The function `sine` below applies the identity until the angle is no larger than 0.1. How many times is `p` applied when `sine(12.15)` is evaluated? What are the orders of growth in space and number of steps used by `sine(a)` as *a* increases? Answer the first question by writing `p_count(angle)`.

<Solution>

**Solution.** 12.15 divided by 3 five times is 0.05, so `p` is applied five times. Each application divides the angle by 3, so `p_count(a)` is about log₃(10*a*): a logarithmic number of steps, and because every `p` waits for the `sine` inside it, logarithmic space too. Multiplying the angle by a million adds only twelve applications.

</Solution>
